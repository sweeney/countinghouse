Consuming the
Price API

Four new JSON routes say when electricity is cheap. They are the only part of this service that can change what a house does rather than report what it did — so the payloads are shaped for a consumer that has to decide, not merely draw.

The routes

Four questions, four endpoints

All four sit behind the same bearer auth as the rest of the API, accept user and service tokens alike — the consumers here are other services as often as dashboards — and send permissive CORS plus Timing-Allow-Origin, so a browser page served from file:// can call them and still read its own timing breakdown.

GET /prices/upcoming
hours=1…48  (default 24)
What is about to happen, and when should I act?
The dashboard endpoint, and the one to reach for first. Carries the forward curve plus every derivation a consumer would otherwise compute — and would compute differently from the next consumer: per-slot band, rank and percentile, the price in force right now, and the cheapest contiguous run at four durations.
GET /prices/cheapest
duration=90m|3h  required
before=RFC3339
When should this specific load run?
One question, one answer. Give it a duration and optionally a deadline; it returns the cheapest contiguous window, or 404 if no window of that length exists in the prices held.
GET /prices
window=today|week|month|custom|Nd|Nh
from, to  (custom only)
What were — or will be — the prices over this window?
The curve over any window, past or future. The archive and the forward curve are the same table, which is the whole point of keeping prices rather than only fetching what is next. Carries known_to and summary.current, so a live display drawing retrospective context can watch for the daily publication from this one call. Capped at 31 days: a row per half hour adds up.
GET /prices/stats
window=…  (same vocabulary)
Is this tariff actually worth the bother?
Per-local-day min, max, mean, spread and plunge-slot count. A row per day, so the cap is 366. spread is the number that says whether shifting load that day was worth doing at all.
I am drawing a chart of the next day and want bands and a “now” marker. → /prices/upcoming
I have a dishwasher, it needs three hours, and it must be done by 07:00. → /prices/cheapest
I want today’s whole curve, or last Tuesday’s, on one axis. → /prices?window=…
I want a sparkline of daily spread over the last three months. → /prices/stats
I want to know what I actually paid, not what it cost per kWh. → /bill  ·  /devices/{id}/cost
Why the payload is shaped this way

The derivations are served, not invented

A raw list of prices would be a smaller response and a worse API. Two dashboards inventing their own definition of “cheap” is how a house ends up with two screens disagreeing about whether now is a good time to put the washing on. So band, rank and percentile come down the wire.

plunge  ≤ 0p cheap  ≤ median − 15% normal peak  ≥ median + 15%

Reading the three derivations

band is the one to render. plunge is absolute — any price at or below zero, whatever the rest of the day looks like — and kept separate from “cheap” because free energy is categorically different from merely inexpensive. The other three are relative to the window’s median, ±15%.

rank is 1 for the cheapest, because the question is always “when should I run this”, never “what is the most expensive half hour”.

percentile is 0 at the cheapest and 1 at the dearest, for consumers that want their own thresholds — and summary.median now carries the centre our bands are actually drawn from, so that invitation is one you can act on. Offering the choice while withholding the figure it turns on was not really an offer; see Five things I changed below.

Bands are window-relative

The same half hour can be normal in one response and peak in another, because the window it was ranked against differs. A 29.2p slot is the cheapest of the next eight hours and an unremarkable middle of the whole day. Never cache a band against a timestamp and reuse it under a different window — cache the price, which does not move, and re-derive.

Draw slots as bars, coloured by band. Do not compute your own thresholds unless you mean to; if you do, use summary.median so you are at least disagreeing with us on purpose.
Mark summary.current. It is the price of the slot covering now, and it is absent when no held slot covers it — which happens in the two-slot tail gap before a publication. Render the absence as “unknown”, never as zero.
Put cheapest["3h"] in the corner. It is the actionable sentence on the whole panel: “cheapest three hours start at 13:00.” A duration that does not fit the window is omitted rather than shortened — a run labelled 3h is always three hours — so check the key exists before reading it.
Believe unit and vat_included, don’t assume them. Pence per kWh, VAT inclusive, on every price route. A dashboard that guessed wrong is out by 5% on every number on screen and looks completely plausible.

Prices are signed

Negative is real and is the point of the tariff. A chart axis anchored at zero, a Math.abs(), or a colour ramp that starts at the minimum will all quietly erase the best hours of the day. The day in the chart above spent five hours below zero.

Recipe

Scheduling a deferrable load

This is the endpoint that justifies the archive. Two of its behaviours are decisions rather than details, and both will bite a consumer that assumes the obvious thing.

GET/prices/cheapest?duration=3h
{
  "from": "2026-09-16T13:00:00+01:00",
  "to":   "2026-09-16T16:00:00+01:00",
  "slots": 6,
  "mean_price": 30.152,
  "searched_to": "2026-09-18T09:30:00+01:00",
  "tariff_code": "E-1R-AGILE-24-10-01-A",
  "unit": "p/kWh", "vat_included": true
}

The three rules

before is a deadline for finishing, not for starting. Ask for three hours before 07:00 and you will never be handed a run starting at 06:00. This is the opposite of how most scheduling parameters read, and it is the only reading that means anything: the point of the deadline is that the load is done.

A run never spans a gap. If the prices held have a hole in them, no candidate window crosses it. Better a 404 than a window we cannot actually price.

A duration that is not a whole number of half hours rounds up. Ask for 80 minutes and you get three slots. The slot is the atom; half of one cannot be reserved.

404 is an answer, not a fault

“No contiguous window of that length exists in the prices held” is a well-formed question with no answer, which is why it is a 404 and not a 400 or a 503. Handle it by retrying after the next publication — not by alerting. Asking for six hours before 07:00 at three in the afternoon, with tomorrow unpublished, is an ordinary 404 that becomes a 200 shortly after four.

Read searched_to with care

It reports how far the search looked, which is a fixed 48 hours ahead — not how far prices are actually held. In the response above it says the 18th while known_to on the sibling route says the 16th. It bounds the search, not the data. This is on the open list below.

The thing consumers get wrong

Incompleteness is the normal state

The supplier publishes tomorrow’s prices in the late afternoon, and the published horizon ends at 23:00 local — so the furthest day held is always missing its final two half hours. For roughly two thirds of every day, that day is today. A consumer that treats complete: false as a fault will be wrong most of the time.

Three fields, three different questions

known_to — the end of the newest slot held for this tariff, which may be well beyond the window you asked for. This is the field to show a user as “prices known until …”, and the one to compare a deadline against before deciding whether an answer is even possible yet. It is null, never a zero date, when the archive holds nothing.

complete — whether every half hour of the window you asked for holds a price. Routinely false. Treat it as “this chart has holes in it”, not as an error.

missing — the actual half-hour starts with no price, listed rather than left to be inferred from a short slots array. Hatch them; do not interpolate across them, and do not let a charting library join the line as though nothing were absent.

Operating them

Polling, caching, and saying no

The curve changes about once a day. A dashboard polls every few seconds. Without conditional requests, each poll ships a 48-slot payload to say that nothing has changed — so every price route carries a strong ETag and Cache-Control: private, max-age=30.

Browser consumers get this free

A plain fetch() uses the browser’s HTTP cache, so within 30 seconds a repeat call never reaches the network, and after that it revalidates with If-None-Match automatically and takes a 304. The existing Auth.get() helper in consumer-demos needs no change — just don’t pass cache: 'no-store', which would throw the benefit away.

Service consumers have to do it themselves: keep the ETag, send it back as If-None-Match, and treat 304 as “what you have is still current”. In Go, that is a stored tag and a header — and it turns a poll loop from kilobytes into a few hundred bytes.

The tag hashes the rendered body, not a timestamp or known_to. That matters more than it sounds: a band can shift because the window slid forward even though no price changed, and a tag keyed on the data alone would serve a stale chart while insisting it was fresh.

When it says no

Recipe

The screen on the kitchen wall

A always-on display: the price over time, a marker on now, and an answer to “is this a good moment to put the dishwasher on”. It is the use case these routes were shaped for, and the one question it raises that the others do not is what happens when the daily publication lands mid-afternoon.

First, a correction worth having

At 09:00 you do not have prices “until about four o’clock”. You have them until 23:00 tonight — around fourteen hours of forward curve, already published yesterday afternoon. The ~16:00 publication does not extend today; it adds tomorrow. So breakfast is the time of day you know most about, and the screen is never short of something to draw.

Draw one span, in three zones

A fixed rolling window — say now − 3h to now + 12h — beats an axis that ends at the horizon, because the horizon jumps by a day at publication and a chart that rescales itself is unreadable at a glance. With a fixed span the axis never moves; what changes is how much of it is filled.

Past, muted and unbanded. You cannot run the dishwasher at 07:00 this morning, so the retrospective third is context, not choice. Draw it greyed. This also sidesteps a trap: band is relative to the window it was computed against, so bands spanning past and future are diluted by history.

Future, banded. These are the options. Colour by band.

Beyond known_to, hatched. Not missing — unpublished. The distinction matters on a screen somebody trusts.

Two calls, two jobs

GETthe drawing call · one consistent band scale across the whole span
/prices?window=custom&from=<now−3h>&to=<now+12h>

// slots[]    — the curve, with band/rank/percentile
// known_to   — where the curve stops: everything after this is hatched
// summary.current — the price right now, for the big number
// summary.median  — the centre, if you want your own thresholds
// complete   — false means there are holes; see below
GETthe verdict call · judged against the choices you actually have
/prices/upcoming?hours=12

// band on the CURRENT slot  — the good/bad light
// cheapest["2h"], ["3h"]    — “cheapest 3h starts 13:00”

Both are separate calls on purpose. The chart wants one band scale across everything visible; the verdict wants bands measured against the future alone, because “is now a good time” means “compared with my remaining options”, and a window padded with this morning answers a different question. Both are ETagged and max-age=30, so the second call costs almost nothing.

For a specific appliance, skip the bands entirely and ask the question directly: /prices/cheapest?duration=3h&before=<bedtime> returns the window to run it in, and the difference between its mean_price and the current price is the saving to put on screen.

The refresh loop

Move the “now” marker with no network at all. A local timer every 30 seconds redraws the dashed line and re-reads which slot covers now — from data you already hold. A screen that fetches in order to move a marker is doing the work of a clock over HTTP.
Fetch for DATA on a schedule. Every five minutes is ample for a curve that changes once a day. Between 15:00 and 16:30 local, drop to once a minute so the screen picks the publication up within a minute of it landing — publication has been observed at 15:54 and 15:55.
Let the browser do the conditional request. A plain fetch() revalidates with If-None-Match and takes a 304 on its own. Most polls cost a header exchange, not 48 slots.
Watch known_to, not the clock. If it moved, the publication landed — refill the hatched zone. If it did not but the body changed, a price was restated: redraw the curve in place. Two different events, distinguishable by one field, and neither needs you to guess what time the supplier publishes.
JSthe whole loop
let knownTo = null;

async function refresh() {
  // Plain fetch: the browser revalidates and takes the 304 by itself.
  const r = await fetch(url(), { headers: { Authorization: `Bearer ${token}` } });
  if (!r.ok) return markStale(r.status);   // keep the last good frame
  const d = await r.json();

  if (d.known_to !== knownTo) {        // the horizon moved: publication
    knownTo = d.known_to;
    refillUnknownZone(d);
  }
  drawCurve(d);                        // slots, bands, summary.current
  markFresh();
}

// Data on a schedule — faster while the publication is due.
const period = () => { const h = new Date().getHours();
                      return (h === 15 || h === 16) ? 60_000 : 300_000; };

// The marker is a clock, not a request.
setInterval(moveNowMarker, 30_000);

Things that will happen to a screen nobody reboots

The other half

What it actually cost

The price routes say what a kWh costs. /bill, /series and /devices/{id}/cost say what you paid — and they gained four fields that a consumer now has to read.

GET/bill?window=today
{
  "cost": 3.5723, "kwh": 10.929, "currency": "GBP",
  "attribution": "counter_slot",   // or "flat_rate"
  "effective_rate": 0.32683,      // GBP/kWh actually paid, inc VAT
  // "unpriced_kwh" is ABSENT here — nothing went unpriced.
  // When present, it is energy we could not price, and it is
  // NOT included in "cost". Surface it; do not add it in.
  "standing_charge": 0.6195,
  "standing_charge_source": "archive"  // or "config"
}

attribution tells you how closely to read the number. flat_rate is kWh × one rate — exact. counter_slot is each half hour’s metered energy priced at that half hour’s own rate. Whole-house figures are exact either way; per-device figures under counter_slot carry a few percent of noise on low-draw continuous devices, because a plug-class counter ticks in 0.1 kWh steps rather than continuously.

effective_rate is the number to put on screen next to a device. On a half-hourly tariff there is no single unit rate to quote, so this is what makes a cost legible — whether the dishwasher ran cheap or dear against the day. It can be negative.

unpriced_kwh is the one to not ignore

It is energy consumed in half hours the archive holds no rate for, it is omitted when zero, and it is deliberately not folded into cost. Its presence always means the answer is incomplete. Charging nothing for real energy is the quiet way a bill comes out wrong, so the field exists to make that visible rather than absorbed — which only works if a consumer renders it.

standing_charge_source says where the daily charge came from. archive is the supplier's own VAT-inclusive figure, so a VAT change arrives in it the same way it arrives in energy prices. config is the configured ex-VAT rate grossed up by the configured VAT rate — correct only while somebody keeps that rate current, and the fallback when no archived charge covers the whole window (a fresh deployment, before the first daily sweep), when the window spans a genuine tariff change, or when the read fails. Nothing to act on in normal operation; worth surfacing in an audit view, because the two have different things that can go wrong with them.

Charts and bills now agree by construction

A /series chart at a one-day interval used to disagree with /bill over the same window by 44%, because a day-wide bucket was priced at whatever the rate happened to be at midnight. Energy is now queried at slot resolution, costed there, and folded up into whatever buckets you asked to see. You can plot cost[] against a bill total and expect them to match.

Reconsidered

Five things I changed for you

Writing this document was the first time I read these routes as a consumer rather than as their author, and three choices did not survive it. Two more came later — one from a dated external event, one from someone describing the screen they wanted on their kitchen wall. Each was defensible in isolation; each cost a reader something. All five were free to change now, because nothing consumes these endpoints yet — which is exactly why now was the moment.

1  ·  The banding centre is now served

percentile was offered so a consumer that dislikes our thresholds could band differently — but the summary carried min, max and mean, and the bands are drawn from the median. The one figure you needed to reproduce or replace our banding was the one absent.

was "summary": { "slots": 16, "min": 29.201,
  "mean": 34.212, "max": 53.991 }
now "summary": { "slots": 16, "min": 29.201,
  "median": 30.686, "mean": 34.212, "max": 53.991 }

The OpenAPI description also said bands sat ±15% from the mean, which was simply wrong — the code has always used the median, and the README said so. Spec and code now agree.

2  ·  /prices/stats uses the same VAT basis as everything else

Every other price route reports VAT-inclusive pence. /prices/stats reported ex-VAT under the same key names, on the grounds that daily aggregates are analytical rather than prices on a screen. That is a real argument and it lost to a simpler one: a dashboard plotting a daily mean against a live price was out by the VAT rate with nothing on the wire to say so, and the difference had been documented in three places — three places describing a surprise instead of one preventing it.

was "vat_included": false
"mean": 37.544  ← ex-VAT
"mean_inc_vat": 39.421
now "vat_included": true
"mean": 39.421  ← matches siblings
"mean_exc_vat": 37.544

Both bases are still emitted, computed independently rather than grossed up — VAT on a negative price makes it more negative. Only which one owns the plain name changed.

3  ·  window=today now means the whole day

Everywhere else in this API, today is period-to-date, because nobody has consumed this evening’s electricity yet. Prices are the exception: today’s are published in full before today begins. Asking for today’s curve at 09:45 returned 20 slots — and reported complete: true, because the window it was measured against also stopped at 09:45.

was — at 09:45 "to": "2026-09-16T09:45:00+01:00"
20 slots
"complete": true  — half a chart, called whole
now — at 09:45 "to": "2026-09-17T00:00:00+01:00"
46 slots
"complete": false  — the whole day, honest about the tail gap

week and month stay period-to-date on purpose. The supplier publishes about a day and a half ahead, so extending those would return a window that is mostly holes and never complete. The asymmetry tracks a real one in the data rather than a tidy rule.

4  ·  A tax change is not a tariff change

This one came from a dated event rather than from reading. HMRC zero-rates domestic electricity in Great Britain for supplies from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027. Expressing that means splitting the agreement into dated blocks — but the blocks differ only in VAT rate, under one unchanged tariff code. The tax changed; the tariff did not.

The boundary refusal was counting agreement blocks, so the moment that configuration lands, every window crossing either boundary would have returned 400 “this window spans a tariff change” — about a window where the tariff is identical throughout. That is every month-spanning price request blacked out for the six months of the zero rate, and again for six months after it ends.

was refuse when the window crosses
any agreement boundary
now refuse when the window crosses a change
of curve identity — the tariff code

Safe for a half-hourly curve specifically because the prices served are the supplier’s own inc-VAT figures out of the archive, never grossed up from the configured rate — so a VAT change simply arrives in the data. A flat tariff across a VAT change is still refused, because flat_price is a single config-derived number that genuinely differs either side, and one field cannot carry both.

5  ·  /prices can now feed a live display on its own

Describing the kitchen screen above surfaced two fields in the wrong place. known_to — the field a consumer polls to see the publication land — was only on /prices/upcoming, which is forward-only. So any display wanting a few hours of retrospective context had to call a second endpoint purely to learn one number.

And summary.current was worse than missing: /prices and /prices/upcoming share the PriceCurveSummary schema, which documents it — so the spec promised a field this route never sent.

was chart + horizon = two calls
summary.current documented, never sent
now chart + horizon = one call
summary.current sent when the window covers now

known_to also joined the ETag, which was not optional: a publication extends the horizon without touching the slots of a past window, so a tag keyed only on that window's content would answer 304 while the field being polled for had moved — the same failure the content fingerprint was introduced to fix.

Not yet settled

Open questions a consumer would notice

searched_to overstates what is known. On /prices/cheapest it reports the search horizon — a flat 48 hours — not how far prices actually run. Adding known_to to that response would let a scheduler tell “there is no cheap window” apart from “tomorrow isn’t published yet”, which are different situations needing different retries.

Omitted cheapest keys are easy to trip over. data.cheapest["3h"].from throws when 3h did not fit the window. Omission is the right call — a run labelled 3h must be three hours — but an explicit {"fits": false} would be kinder than absence.

No push. Everything here is polled. The publication is a discrete daily event that consumers actively wait for, and a lifecycle event over MQTT would suit it better than five-minute polling from 15:00. Deliberately parked, with the reasoning recorded.

No /prices demo page yet. The ten existing consumer-demos pages cover energy, cost and climate; none of them draws a price curve. Until one does, these routes have documentation but no worked consumer — and a worked consumer is what actually finds the awkward parts, as the three changes above rather demonstrate.
