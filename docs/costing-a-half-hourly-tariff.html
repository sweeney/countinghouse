Costing a
Half-Hourly Tariff

A home electricity bill used to be one multiplication. On a tariff that re-prices every thirty minutes — sometimes below zero — it stops being one, and almost every assumption downstream of that multiplication turns out to have been load-bearing.

01

Why a bill stops being a multiplication

Countinghouse reads per-device telemetry out of InfluxDB and answers questions about energy and money over arbitrary windows. Until this change, every cost it produced was the same expression:

cost = kWh × unit_rate × (1 + vat_rate)

That works because a fixed tariff has exactly one price. The expression has nowhere to put a second one, and — more quietly — nowhere to put "I don't know."

An Agile tariff prices electricity per half-hour settlement period, tracking the wholesale day-ahead market. Forty-eight prices a day, published the afternoon before, and on a windy low-demand afternoon they go negative: the grid pays you to take the electricity. The chart above is one such day.

So the multiplication needs three things it cannot express: a rate that varies within the window, a rate that might be unknown for some of it, and a rate that can be below zero. The last is the easiest to get right and the easiest to forget — VAT on a negative price makes it more negative, not less.

The shape of the work

Record every price for perpetuity; verify what arrives before trusting it; use it to cost real consumption per half hour; and publish the forward curve so other services can move load into the cheap slots. Everything else in this document follows from those four.

02

Terms

Defined once, used throughout. Several are ordinary words used in a narrow sense, which is exactly when a glossary earns its place.

Slothalf-hour settlement period
The atom of this entire system: a 30-minute window with one price. A local day holds 48 of them — except across a daylight-saving changeover, where it holds 46 or 50. Every interval here is half-open, [from, to), so the boundary instant belongs to the later slot.
Tariff codeE-1R-AGILE-24-10-01-A
The supplier's identifier, parsed rather than pattern-matched: {fuel}-{registers}R-{product}-{region}. The trailing letter is the grid supply point — one of fourteen UK distribution regions — and it changes the price. The standing charge alone differs by 57% between regions, so a mix-up is a silently wrong bill rather than a 404.
Bitemporaltwo independent time axes
Every archived price carries two times, and conflating them is the classic archive bug. Valid time is when the price applied — the half hour it governs. Transaction time is when we learned it. A price for Tuesday evening, learned on Monday afternoon, revised on Tuesday morning, has one valid time and two transaction times.
retrieved_attransaction time, as stored
When the archive last confirmed a price — not when it first saw it. A re-read that agrees still moves it forward. That choice is deliberate and it matters for restatements; §10 covers what it cost.
Restatementa price that changed after we stored it
The supplier publishing a different value for a half hour we had already recorded — possibly already billed. The archive is append-only with a separate restatement log precisely so "has any price we billed ever changed?" is answerable later, not only at the moment it happens. Observed so far: none.
Horizonhow far the feed reaches
The end of the newest slot the supplier has published. One request of about 350 bytes answers it, which is what makes polling for a new publication cheap.
Tail gapthe furthest day is always short
The published horizon stops at 23:00 local, so the furthest day held is always missing its final two half hours. Not a fault — the feed's normal shape. §9 is the measurement, and the trouble it caused.
Sweepa deliberate re-read of days already held
A routine sync stops as soon as the horizon stops moving, so nothing ever re-reads a day it already has — and a restatement would go unnoticed. A sweep re-reads the last fortnight nightly. Finding nothing is nearly free: every unchanged slot comes back unchanged.
known_to / complete_totwo different questions
known_to is the end of the newest slot held. complete_to is the end of the newest fully populated local day. They differ, and the difference is load-bearing: a publication can push the horizon across a whole day while leaving that day short, so known_to alone answers "do we have tomorrow?" with yes when the answer is no.
Attributioncounter_slot | flat_rate
How a cost was arrived at, reported on the wire because the method changes how closely a figure should be read. flat_rate: kWh × one rate, exact. counter_slot: each half hour's metered energy priced at that half hour's own rate.
unpriced_kwhenergy we cannot price
Consumption in a half hour the archive holds no rate for. Reported separately and deliberately not folded into the cost, because charging nothing for real energy is the quiet way a bill comes out wrong. Absent when zero, so its presence always means the answer is incomplete.
Counter quantisation0.1 kWh steps
Plug-class devices report a cumulative hardware counter that ticks in 0.1 kWh increments. A 67 W fridge takes about 90 minutes to accumulate one tick, so its per-slot energy is a series of spikes rather than a smooth draw. §8 is about what that does to a per-device price.
Plunge pricingat or below zero
Slots where electricity is free or paid-for. The entire point of the tariff, and the reason a validation gate must never reject a "surprising" price: it would discard exactly the slots worth knowing about.
Effective ratecost ÷ priced kWh
What was actually paid per kWh, VAT inclusive. On a half-hourly tariff there is no single unit rate to report, so this is the number that makes a cost readable — whether a device ran cheap or dear against the day. It can be negative.
03

The defect that started it

A half-hourly tariff has no unit rate in configuration. It cannot: the price varies within the agreement, so there is no single number to put there, and a "representative" one would be read as the price and be wrong. The field is therefore zero, and the rate lives in the archive.

Which means the old expression did not fail on such a tariff. It returned:

cost = 12.4 kWh × 0 × 1.05
     = £0.00

Defect · silent zero

The moment the tariff configuration was switched over, /bill, /series and /devices/{id}/cost would all have reported £0.00 for real consumption. Not an error. Not an empty response. A confident zero in the shape of a correct answer — the worst available outcome, because nothing about it invites a second look.

The fix is a type that can say "I don't know":

type Pricer interface {
    // The bool is load-bearing: false means NO PRICE IS KNOWN,
    // which a caller must surface as unpriced energy rather than
    // treat as free. Returning (0, true) would be a lie, and it is
    // the specific lie this interface exists to prevent.
    RateAt(t time.Time) (float64, bool)
}

Everything in §7 follows from making unknown-ness expressible.

04

The pipeline

Five stages, with one governing rule: fail open on fetching, fail loud on pricing. A fetch that does not work leaves the archive exactly as it was and records why — it never writes a guess, because a plausible wrong price is worse than a visible gap. A gap that will affect billing is pushed at somebody rather than left on a health endpoint for whenever they next look.

01 · detect Probe the horizon One ~350-byte request asks how far the feed reaches. If it hasn't moved, nothing else runs.
02 · fetch Pull new slots From the last known horizon, with two hours of overlap so a slot missed at a boundary is picked up rather than skipped forever.
03 · verify Three gates Structure, plausibility, completeness. Only the first can reject a row. See §6.
04 · store Archive SQLite, idempotent, append-only, with the engine enforcing the key and a separate restatement log.
05 · serve Cost & publish Per-slot costing behind /bill, and the forward curve behind four /prices routes.

Scheduling

Work and scheduling are separated: one function does a unit of work, another says when to come back, and both are pure functions of the clock and the archive. That split is what lets a test drive an entire publication day — including a late publication, and one that never arrives — deterministically and in microseconds.

Prices for the next day appear after 16:00, so the collector watches every five minutes from 15:00, and otherwise idles at hourly. A sweep runs at 02:00.

05

The archive, and two kinds of time

This is the one thing the service writes. Everything else it knows is derived on demand from InfluxDB and survives a restart with no state at all; the price archive cannot be, because it is not rebuildable from Influx. It is rebuildable from the supplier today, and the entire reason to keep it is the day that stops being true.

The key, enforced by the engine

The primary key is (tariff_code, payment_method, valid_from). The payment method is in there because a variable tariff splits into direct-debit and non-direct-debit rows for the same slot: without it, the second row looks like a restatement of the first and fires a false alarm.

valid_to is nullable, because the supplier genuinely omits it on an open-ended row, and inventing an end is a different statement from not knowing one.

Two relations, not one

The supplier's daily standing charge is archived the same way, in its own table with the same key and its own restatement log. A separate table rather than a kind column, because the two hold different units — pence per day against pence per kWh — and one table makes it possible to sum them with a query that forgot to filter. The Go types are separate for the same reason and convert explicitly; the storage path is shared, so the bitemporal machinery has one implementation to be right.

It was the last piece to land, and the reason it stopped being optional is the subject of §12: while the standing charge was computed from configuration, it was the only number left in a bill grossed up from a rate a human has to keep current.

Why SQLite, not the time-series database

The obvious answer is Influx — the service already reads it. The argument for it was that prices and energy could then be joined in one query. That benefit turned out to be illusory: no money logic lives in the Influx layer at all, so there was no join to be had. What remained was that prices are relational facts with a compound key, a nullable field, restatement history and an audit requirement, which is a row store's job. Roughly 225 bytes a slot — measured at 7.8 MB for two years of one tariff.

06

Three gates, differing in what a failure costs

Validation is not one check with one severity. What a failure should cost depends entirely on what failed, and getting that wrong in either direction is expensive: too strict and you discard correct data, too lax and you bill on nonsense.

The VAT check moved gates, and that was the interesting decision

The supplier sends each price twice — excluding and including VAT. Checking that they agree catches two disasters in one assertion: a VAT change nobody told us about, and a unit error (pence read as pounds) in either column.

It was originally a Gate A rejection. That is wrong, and the argument is worth stating because it generalises. Both columns come from the supplier and are self-consistent with each other; what disagrees is our own configuration's VAT rate. And no unit price is ever derived from that rate — the cost path reads the supplier's delivered inc-VAT figure directly. So rejecting the slot discarded correct data to protect an assumption nothing downstream reads.

The case that settled it — and it has a date

On a real VAT change, Gate A would have refused every slot published after it until somebody edited configuration. The window in which prices matter most would be the window with no prices — while the health endpoint cheerfully reported fetches succeeding.

This stopped being hypothetical while the work was in progress. HMRC zero-rates domestic electricity in Great Britain for supplies from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027, then back to 5%. Under the original Gate A rejection, the archive would have emptied itself on the morning of the 1st and again on 1 April — twice, on schedule, for a change everybody knew was coming.

One further thing the warning now does, because a warning nobody reads is the failure this service already learned once: when a whole batch implies one consistent rate that is not the configured one, that is a rate change rather than a data blip, and it escalates to an alert naming the rate the supplier is actually charging. Slots disagreeing in different directions stay per-slot warnings — that is a data problem, and paging for it would train somebody to ignore the page.

It is now a Gate B warning carrying the implied rate (inc ÷ exc − 1), which is both what an operator needs in order to correct configuration and — since it is recorded per slot by construction — most of what a per-slot VAT column would have bought.

07

Costing, and the axis that wasn't

With a pricer that can say "I don't know", costing a window becomes: take each half hour's metered energy, price it at that half hour's own rate, and sum. A window spanning a tariff change splits into segments, each with its own rate and its own VAT multiplier — not a corner case, since the first bill after any switchover necessarily is one.

Then a reviewer asked what happens when the bucket is wider than the price.

Defect · the cost axis

A chart does not ask for 48 buckets a day. The default interval is 1 hour for a day-long window and 1 day for a month. Pricing a bucket at the rate holding at its start is exact when the axes line up and silently wrong otherwise — a one-day bucket charged the whole day at its midnight rate.

The fix, and the fix that was rejected

The tempting cheap answer is to spread a coarse bucket's energy evenly across its slots. That is exact for a fridge and badly wrong for a dishwasher — which is precisely the load a half-hourly tariff exists to shift. So it was rejected.

Instead the energy is queried at slot resolution, costed there, and the costs folded up into whatever buckets the caller asked to see. kwh[] keeps the requested resolution, cost[] is exact, and the chart agrees with the bill by construction rather than at one particular interval.

A flat tariff skips all of it: the price cannot change inside a bucket, so a monthly chart stays one query per day rather than 1,488. That is decided by an optional interface a pricer implements to declare how fast it varies — flat rates decline, and declining means "treat me as constant".

Folding maps fine buckets to display buckets by timestamp, never by a fixed ratio, because a local day is 46, 48 or 50 half hours. A ratio would misalign every bucket after a changeover — on the one day of the year nobody checks.

08

Whose kWh was it, and when?

Whole-house cost under this tariff is exact: the meter is fine-grained and is the billing reference. Per-device cost is not, and the reason is the counter.

Three options, and why the noisy one won

The alternatives were to take the shape from the power series and the magnitude from the counter, or to abandon per-device pricing and apportion the house total. Pricing each counter delta at its own slot's rate was chosen because of where its noise lands: it is worst for low-draw continuous devices, whose consumption cannot be shifted anyway, and it stays clean for the deferrable loads — dishwasher, washing machine, tumble dryer — where the number is actually actionable. Roughly 6:1 signal to noise, measured over a month.

It also sums exactly to the monitored house cost, by construction, rather than approximately.

Measured, and it overturned a hypothesis

The continuous devices' small positive bias was suspected to be an artefact — the counter reporting each tick late, pushing energy into later and dearer slots. It is not. Removing quantisation entirely, by pricing the unquantised power shape instead, leaves the bias intact. These devices genuinely do consume more in dearer half hours, because compressor duty tracks ambient temperature and temperature and the evening peak share a daily shape.

09

The tail gap

The archive holds 729 local days. 724 of them are exactly complete. The only short day is always the furthest one published, and it is always missing precisely its final two half hours.

Why it mattered

The collector treated an incomplete today as a fault. It is not — it is what a healthy feed looks like for two thirds of every day. Worse, complete_to collapsed to the zero time whenever today was short, which the health endpoint read as "no complete day of prices held" — about an archive holding two years of them.

A tail gap within two slots is now tolerated on either day. An interior hole still pages at any size — that means the supplier published a slot that never reached us, which is a real fault. Telling those two apart is the whole distinction.

10

What running it found that reading it didn't

Four defects in this work were found by data rather than by inspection. They are listed together because the pattern is the point: each was invisible to a test suite that only ever constructs fresh state.

Found by a reviewer reading carefully

The second pricing loop. The rest-of-home decomposition had its own copy of the costing code. It was fixed in one place and not the other — so a month's unmonitored cost was understated by a third, and missing prices rounded away to an unpriced_kwh of exactly zero, which reads as "nothing was missing". Three adversarial tests existed and all three pointed at the copy that had been fixed.

Found by running it against the live feed

The alert that never clears. §9. Two days of production logging turned an argument into a count.

Found by looking at the archive the morning after

The sweep erases its own evidence. The nightly sweep reported restated=0 inserted=0 — which reads as "did nothing" — having rewritten 716 rows' retrieved_at to its own timestamp. That is deliberate: retrieved_at means last confirmed, which gives a restatement a tighter bound. But nothing had ever re-read held days before, so the column had incidentally recorded first seen — and that is exactly what the publication schedule in §9 was measured from. Both facts are wanted, so they need two columns. Not yet built.

Found by upgrading a dependency

Two versions behind. A shared library's database-copy routine was believed unsafe and worked around locally. It had been fixed two releases earlier; the belief came from reading a checked-out copy rather than the version the build resolves. Bumping it deleted 155 lines of local workaround and picked up three security changes that had been sitting unused.

The common thread is that none of these was a logic error visible in the diff. Each was a mismatch between what the code was believed to do and what it did when something real was pointed at it.

12

What is still open

Decisions, not work

What complete_to should mean when tomorrow is tail-short. Advancing it makes it equal known_to in the routine case, collapsing a distinction the health block exists to draw.

Two columns for two times — an immutable first_seen_at beside the mutable retrieved_at, per §10.

Known gaps

The tariff code is still trusted. Nothing cross-checks the configured tariff code against the supplier's own record of which tariff this account is on, so a stale document would price the wrong tariff and nothing would look unhealthy. The VAT half of that gap is now closed — a systematic disagreement between the supplier's figures and the configured rate raises an alert — but the code itself is still taken on faith.

An untested restore. The archive is backed up offsite and the upload is verified; nothing has yet been restored from it. A backup that has not been restored is a hypothesis.

Deliberately parked

Lifecycle events over MQTT, export tariffs, and multi-site — each recorded with the original reasoning so they can be picked up rather than re-derived.

Per-appliance run energy — the figure that would let a price display say “save 27p” rather than “save 12p/kWh”. Parked because it sits outside this service's boundary rather than beyond its ability: countinghouse answers what a window cost, from meters, past tense. What a dishwasher will draw is a forward statement about a load, and statehouse already publishes it — the appliance_cycle measurement carries per-cycle selected_energy_kwh and integrated_kwh (AGENT_BRIEF §3). A consumer wanting money rather than unit rates should read that, or assume a fixed kWh per cycle and be most of the way there.

Forty-eight half hours: ten below zero, six at peak, and the last two not yet published. One day, and the unit everything above is built on.
